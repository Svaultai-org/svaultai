A responsible digital inheritance planning checklist

Digital inheritance planning means deciding what should happen to selected accounts, files, and records if you can no longer manage them. The goal is continuity without giving away unnecessary access today.

Important: a digital-vault plan can organize information, but it is not a will and does not replace legal, estate, tax, or financial advice. Requirements differ by location and asset type.

1. Make an inventory without oversharing

List the categories that matter: financial records, domains, subscriptions, creative work, family media, device information, and instructions. Do not automatically include every password. Some services have their own legacy-contact or transfer rules that should be followed.

2. Choose the right trusted person

A beneficiary should understand the responsibility and know that a plan exists. Consider whether one person should receive everything or whether different people should handle family records, business systems, and financial matters.

3. Define release conditions clearly

Decide what must happen before selected information becomes available. Avoid vague instructions. Review contact details and make sure the trusted person can still be reached through the method the system expects.

4. Minimize what is stored

Share only what the beneficiary genuinely needs. A concise instruction that points to an official executor process may be safer than storing unrestricted access to an account. Separate sentimental material from credentials and regulated records.

5. Review the plan regularly

  • Remove closed accounts and outdated instructions.
  • Update beneficiary contact information after major life changes.
  • Confirm that important files still open correctly.
  • Check whether a provider's transfer rules have changed.
  • Make sure the plan still matches your legal documents.
